# Inference-Engine Kernels and Serving Systems: Consolidated Memo (evidence cutoff 2026-10-19)

**Scope and labels.** This memo consolidates several overlapping drafts of one targeted, non-systematic review; no new research was performed. Sourced findings are stated plainly with citations. **Derivation** marks my inference; **Recommendation** marks judgment. I read full papers except where noted: DeepSeek-V3 only its infrastructure and inference sections (§§3.2–3.4), and Ring Attention in full HTML but only its training experiments. Vendor documentation is identified as such, not treated as independent evaluation. Speedups appear only where hardware, workload, comparator, and metric were stated; several draft numbers were dropped because one of those conditions was missing. Retained inconsistencies are flagged rather than silently resolved.

## 1. Attention kernels

**FlashAttention (2022)** reframed attention as an I/O problem. It tiles Q/K/V through SRAM, uses online softmax, and recomputes attention in the backward pass instead of materializing the N×N score matrix. The paper argues Θ(N²d²/M) HBM accesses versus Θ(Nd+N²) for standard attention, with linear extra memory and exact output [1]. This is a training-era kernel/algorithm contribution; it says nothing about paged KV, continuous batching, or decode.

**FlashAttention-2 (2023)** fixed work partitioning: fewer non-matmul FLOPs, parallelism over sequence length as well as batch/heads, and warp-level splitting that avoids split-K communication. On A100 it reports roughly 2× over FA1 and 50–73% of theoretical peak [2]. It remains synchronous and Ampere-oriented.

**FlashAttention-3 (2024)** is Hopper-specific: warp-specialized producer/consumer pipelines, asynchronous WGMMA and TMA, GEMM–softmax overlap, and FP8 with block quantization plus incoherent processing. On H100 SXM5, FP16, sequence lengths 512–16k at 16k total tokens and head dimensions 64/128/256, it reports 1.5–2.0× over FA2 and up to 740 TFLOP/s (75% utilization); FP8 approaches 1.2 PFLOP/s with lower error than per-tensor scaling [3]. Caveats: H100-only, attention-only, and FP8 parity depends on head dimension and causal masking.

**FlashAttention-4 (2026)** addresses Blackwell’s asymmetric scaling: tensor-core throughput doubled while shared-memory bandwidth and exponential-unit throughput did not, so softmax and shared-memory traffic dominate. It uses fully asynchronous MMA, larger tiles, TMEM, 2-CTA MMA, software-emulated exponentials, conditional softmax rescaling, and a CuTe-DSL implementation. On B200, BF16, 1k–32k sequences at 32k total tokens, head dimensions 64/128/(192,128), it reports up to 1.3× over cuDNN 9.13, 2.7× over Triton, and 1613 TFLOP/s (71%) [4]. Two caveats: the appendix names a B100 180GB benchmark platform while the abstract says B200, and newer cuDNN versions have absorbed some techniques. Code is released, but I found no verification that vLLM, SGLang, or TensorRT-LLM ship FA4 as a default backend.

**FlashInfer (MLSys 2025)** is the serving-oriented complement: a unified block-sparse-row layout covering paged, ragged, tree, and block-sparse KV; composable formats for shared prefixes; JIT-generated attention variants; and a load-balanced scheduler compatible with CUDA graphs. It is integrated into SGLang, vLLM, and MLC-Engine [5]. On SGLang v0.3.4, Llama 3.1 8B/70B, A100/H100, under a P99 TTFT target, it reports 29–69% lower inter-token latency than the Triton backend, plus 28–30% lower long-context latency; sparse gather costs about 10% on prefill. These are version- and integration-specific results.

**Derivation.** Kernel peak is a weak predictor of serving throughput. Prefill is compute/softmax-bound and benefits from FA3/FA4-class pipelines; decode is bandwidth-bound, has query length one, and depends more on paged/varlen addressing, split-K/FlashDecoding work partitioning, launch overhead, and CUDA-graph stability. PagedAttention’s indirection measured 20–26% higher attention-kernel latency than a contiguous kernel while still winning end-to-end by enabling larger batches [7].

## 2. Batching, paging, and prefix reuse

**Orca (OSDI 2022)** introduced iteration-level scheduling and selective batching. The scheduler invokes the engine one iteration at a time, admits arrivals and retires finished requests between iterations, batches non-attention operations, splits attention per request, and reserves KV slots up to `max_tokens`. It uses FCFS with no preemption. On GPT-3 175B, 16×A100-80GB, with a synthetic Poisson trace, it reports 36.9× throughput over FasterTransformer at matched median normalized latency [6]. The number is tied to an obsolete baseline; the durable contribution is the scheduling abstraction.

**vLLM/PagedAttention (SOSP 2023)** replaced contiguous per-request KV allocation with fixed-size blocks and a logical-to-physical block table, enabling on-demand allocation, copy-on-write sharing for parallel sampling/beam search, and prefix sharing. The paper reports that prior systems used only 20.4–38.2% of KV memory for token states; block size 16 is the default compromise. It reports 2–4× throughput over Orca/FasterTransformer on OPT/LLaMA with ShareGPT/Alpaca traces at similar latency [7]. The kernel itself is 20–26% slower than a contiguous optimized kernel—the price of memory flexibility.

**vLLM V1 (2025–2026)** is a re-architecture. The scheduler treats prompt and output tokens uniformly as a dictionary `{request_id: num_tokens}`, making chunked prefill, prefix caching, and speculative decoding variants of one token-budget mechanism. Chunked prefill is default whenever possible; prefix caching is default with near-zero overhead at a 0% hit rate in the project’s benchmark; persistent batches, incremental TP diffs, `torch.compile`, and piecewise CUDA graphs cut CPU overhead. V0 is deprecated and GPU↔CPU KV swapping was removed, so preemption is recompute-oriented and memory pressure raises tail latency through recomputation rather than swap [16][17].

**SGLang/RadixAttention (2023–2024)** treats KV as a radix-tree LRU cache across requests and calls, with longest-shared-prefix-first cache-aware scheduling and frontend fork/join hints. It reports up to 6.4× throughput on prefix-heavy LM-program workloads on A10G/A100, with radix-management overhead below 0.3%; the vLLM baseline was v0.2.5, so the comparison is dated [8]. Production Chatbot Arena hit rates were 52.4% (LLaVA-NeXT-34B) and 74.1% (Vicuna-33B). The paper itself notes that greedy cache-aware scheduling can starve requests and that its DFS optimality result is offline and assumes cache capacity at least the maximum request length.

**SGLang HiCache (2025–2026)** extends this to L1 GPU, L2 host DRAM, and L3 distributed storage (Mooncake, HF3FS/3FS, NIXL, AIBrix, LMCache, file). HiRadixTree records which tier holds each span; prefetch policies are `best_effort`, `wait_complete`, and `timeout`; write-back is write-through, selective, or on-eviction. L2 is node-local and instance-private, so cross-instance reuse requires L3. MLA models need a separate write-back path because every rank holds identical KV. Cache size and hit rate are nonlinear, and page size trades metadata efficiency against partial-page hits [19].

**Derivation.** These are complementary layers: Orca is the scheduling abstraction, vLLM the memory allocator, SGLang the reuse policy. Prefix caching pays only when routing preserves affinity or a shared L3 exists; otherwise the cache is cold and its metadata is pure overhead.

## 3. Scheduling and disaggregation

**Sarathi-Serve (OSDI 2024)** names the central tradeoff: prefill-prioritizing schedulers cause “generation stalls” and TBT spikes; decode-prioritizing schedulers waste throughput. It chunks prefills and builds stall-free hybrid batches under a token budget derived from the TBT SLO, so new prefills piggyback on decode batches without pausing them. Versus vLLM under stated P99 TBT SLOs, it reports 2.6× capacity for Mistral-7B on one A100, 3.7× for Yi-34B on two A100s, and 5.6× for Falcon-180B with TP4-PP2 on eight A100s [9]. Caveats: chunking adds up to ~25% prefill overhead at chunk 512; tile quantization made chunk 257 ~32% slower than 256 in their example; and the artifact is a research fork, not feature-complete vLLM.

**DistServe (OSDI 2024)** makes the goodput argument: prefill is compute-bound and TTFT-sensitive, decode is memory-bound and TPOT-sensitive, and colocation couples their parallelism and creates interference. It assigns separate pools, searches per-phase TP/PP and replica counts, and uses pull-based KV transfer. On OPT-13B/66B/175B, A100-80GB, a 25 Gbps cross-node testbed, Poisson arrivals, and 90% attainment, it reports 7.4× higher request rate or 12.6× tighter SLO versus vLLM/DeepSpeed-MII [10]. Limits: FCFS, no preemption or fault tolerance, and KV transfer below 0.1% of latency only with node-affinity placement and NVLink. The working file cites both an OSDI PDF and arXiv:2401.09670; I treat them as the same paper.

**Splitwise (ISCA 2024)** broadens the argument to cluster design: prompt machines are compute-heavy, token machines memory/power-heavy, and a mixed pool absorbs fragmentation; layer-wise KV transfer overlaps with prompt computation. Using Azure production traces, an event-driven simulator, and two-machine vLLM experiments on A100/H100, it reports throughput/cost gains, but the cluster-level results are simulated and the headline cost comparison is not fully specified [11]. Heterogeneous pools add operational complexity.

**Mooncake (FAST 2025)** is the production counterpoint. Kimi separates prefill and decode clusters but also pools CPU DRAM, SSD, and RDMA NICs into a distributed KV cache; a KVCache-aware Conductor schedules by prefix locality and load; chunked pipeline parallelism scales long-context prefill. On 16×8 A800 nodes with real traces it reports 59–498% higher effective request capacity; in production A800/H800 clusters, 115%/107% more requests at over 100B tokens/day. It recommends ≥100 Gbps for the global cache and rejects requests with HTTP 429 when SLOs cannot be met [12]. Caveats: proprietary traces and model; long-context-heavy workload; a dummy LLaMA3-70B is used for reproducibility.

**AMPD (February 2026; v2 July 2026)** targets multi-round/agentic traffic where incremental prefills interleave with decode. It adaptively runs each prefill locally on the decode worker (no transfer, but pausing decode) or remotely on a prefill worker, reorders the prefill queue, and solves an ILP for deployment. Built on Dynamo/NIXL, on four 8×H20-96GB servers with 200 Gbps IB and Llama3.1-70B/Qwen3-32B/Mixtral-8x7B, it reports 67.29–339.74% average SLO-attainment improvement over colocated and disaggregated baselines [15]. This is a paper prototype, and the metric is trace-specific SLO attainment, not universal throughput.

**Derivation.** Disaggregation is latency isolation, not a throughput free lunch. vLLM’s own documentation says disaggregated prefill “DOES NOT improve throughput” and positions it for TTFT/ITL isolation and tail control [18]. It pays when TTFT and TPOT genuinely conflict, prompts are long, reuse is high, and KV transfer is cheap relative to prefill; chunked prefill is the cheaper default.

## 4. Parallelism and collectives

**Tensor parallelism (TP)** shards each layer and needs an all-reduce per attention/FFN block; it is latency-efficient inside an NVLink domain and poor across commodity nodes. **Pipeline parallelism (PP)** shards layers with point-to-point activations, scales capacity across nodes, and creates bubbles; Sarathi’s uniform token budgets reduce them, and TensorRT-LLM’s in-flight batching requires context-phase sequences to precede generation-phase sequences in the packed input tensor [20]. **Data parallelism (DP)** replicates the model; the hard part is routing. KV-cache-aware routing (Dynamo Smart Router, llm-d, Mooncake Conductor) trades prefix affinity against load, and round-robin routing destroys hit rates while pure affinity overloads hot replicas.

**Expert parallelism (EP)** is now first-class for MoE. DeepSeek-V3 (671B total, 37B active, MLA, one shared plus 256 routed experts, 8 active per token, at most 4 nodes per token) uses TP4 + sequence parallelism, with DP8/EP32 for prefill (4 nodes, 32 GPUs) and DP80/EP320 for decode (40 nodes, 320 GPUs), plus redundant experts rebalanced roughly every 10 minutes [13]. Cross-node EP in training was near 1:1 compute-to-communication; inference all-to-all is the dominant constraint. This is a model-specific recipe with a 40-node minimum decode unit, not a default.

**Context parallelism (CP)** splits the sequence. Ring Attention distributes query blocks and rotates KV blocks around a ring, overlapping transfer with blockwise attention/FFN; context length scales linearly with devices, with block size constrained by FLOPS/bandwidth [14]. The paper reports 32M tokens on 32 A100s and 131M on TPUv4-512, but its experiments are training. CP is mainly a long-prefill TTFT tool; decode CP is harder because KV sharding adds per-token communication and interacts badly with attention DP.

**Collectives.** NCCL remains the default for TP/PP/EP; KV transfer uses NIXL/UCX/RDMA or vendor engines (Mooncake, Ascend). Orca’s separation of control-plane gRPC from NCCL data-plane remains a useful rule: control messages should not serialize on GPU collectives [6].

## 5. Five engines and their limits

| Engine (status) | Core design | Genuine strengths | Constraints / failure modes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **vLLM V1** (released; V0 deprecated) | PagedAttention, unified token-budget scheduler, chunked prefill and prefix caching default, TP, piecewise CUDA graphs | Broad model/hardware coverage, strong continuous batching, low CPU overhead, easy OpenAI-compatible serving | Disaggregation experimental and not a throughput win; no GPU↔CPU swap, so recompute preemption; prefix locality depends on routing; CPU overhead still matters for small models [16][17][18] |
| **SGLang** (released) | RadixAttention, cache-aware scheduling, HiCache L1/L2/L3, PD/EPD disaggregation, structured output, large-scale EP | Best prefix reuse for multi-turn/agentic/RAG; hierarchical cache; DeepSeek-scale MoE | Greedy cache-aware scheduling can starve; L2 is instance-private; L3 correctness/latency depends on backend; PD adds transfer, timeout, and role-configuration complexity [8][19] |
| **TensorRT-LLM** (released) | Compiled engines, in-flight batching, paged KV, chunked context, FP8/FP4, XQA, NIXL disaggregation | Deep NVIDIA kernel/quantization control; strong TTFT/TPOT tuning; heterogeneous TP/PP per phase | NVIDIA-only; build/tuning burden; packed-input ordering; disaggregation is decoder-only with homogeneous per-layer KV; mixed context/generation scheduling is not optimal; unset `cache_transceiver_config` silently disables disagg, and transfer-setting mismatches fail at runtime [20][21] |
| **llama.cpp** (released) | GGUF, CPU/GPU offload, continuous batching default, unified KV buffer, slots, prompt cache/checkpoints, speculative decoding | Edge/desktop and hybrid CPU+GPU; broad quantization; simple deployment | Single-node; no PagedAttention block table or cluster PD; slot-based parallelism; unified KV and context shift have different failure modes; throughput ceiling under high concurrency [22] |
| **ONNX Runtime GenAI** (preview API) | Dynamic batching, paged KV, prefix caching, transactional engine, per-turn cancellation, hybrid paged/fixed state | Portable ONNX execution; explicit cancellation/rollback; works across execution providers | Single-owner thread; exactly one paged-KV group; beam search rejected; static fallback lacks the transactional path; prefix hits require full blocks plus parent identity; not a cluster scheduler [23] |

**Orchestration is not inference.** NVIDIA Dynamo (March 2025) adds a Planner, KV-aware Smart Router, distributed KV-cache manager, and NIXL transport above TensorRT-LLM/vLLM/SGLang/PyTorch; llm-d (May 2025) is the Kubernetes-native equivalent with vLLM and Inference Gateway. They solve placement, routing, tiering, and autoscaling, but their gains depend on connector maturity, network, cache backend, routing quality, and “projected performance” vendor caveats [24][25].

## 6. Released versus prototype

Released and documented: vLLM V1 (V0 deprecated; chunked prefill and prefix caching default; NIXL, Mooncake, LMCache, offloading connectors), SGLang HiCache and PD, TensorRT-LLM IFB and NIXL disaggregation, llama.cpp continuous batching, ONNX RT GenAI dynamic batching, Dynamo, and llm-d. Research prototypes: Sarathi-Serve (fork), DistServe, Splitwise, and AMPD. Mooncake is production at Kimi but its full stack is not a drop-in OSS engine. FA4 is the 2026 Blackwell kernel paper; FA3 is the Hopper reference. I found no independently verified 2026 evidence that one engine dominates all workloads.

## 7. Architecture interfaces

A swappable serving stack needs explicit contracts:

1. **Request:** OpenAI-compatible chat/completions plus token-in/token-out; streaming; request ID stable across prefill and decode; cancellation; priority/deadline or SLO class; cache hint; tenant and idempotency keys.
2. **Scheduler:** token budget (`max_num_tokens`/`max_scheduled_tokens`), max batch size, chunk size, prefill/decode pool selection, admission/rejection (429 with retry-after), FCFS versus priority, preemption (recompute vs swap), and KV reservation policy (SGLang’s `prefill_complete` versus `early`).
3. **KV/cache:** page/block size, block table, prefix hash plus parent-block identity (hash alone is insufficient), tier L1/L2/L3, `put/get/query/evict`, eviction, replication, TTL, tenant isolation, and MLA versus MHA layout.
4. **Transfer:** connector abstraction (NIXL, Mooncake, LMCache, custom), layout transformation for heterogeneous TP/PP, timeout, retry, backpressure, idempotent transfer IDs, and asynchronous overlap with compute.
5. **Execution:** attention backend (FA2/FA3/FA4/FlashInfer/XQA), CUDA graphs or piecewise capture, compile mode, quantized KV, speculative decoding, structured output.
6. **Observability and failure:** per-request queue, prefill, decode, TTFT/TPOT/ITL histograms, cache-hit tokens, transfer time, preemption count, SLO attainment and goodput, joined by request ID. Define cancellation, timeout, partial-KV, duplicate-ID, and whether the engine rolls back or fails stop; ONNX’s dynamic path is transactional before publication and fail-stop after [23].

## 8. Evaluation

**Metrics.** TTFT is arrival to first generated token; state whether tokenization, preprocessing, queueing, and network are included. TPOT is `(last_token_time − first_token_time)/(output_tokens − 1)`, but report the full ITL/TBT distribution because TPOT hides stalls. Report p50/p95/p99 with sample size and estimator (nearest-rank versus interpolated); p99 on a few hundred requests is noisy. SLO-goodput is the maximum rate meeting both TTFT and TPOT at a stated attainment (e.g., 90% or 99%), per GPU; publish the full attainment curve, not one point. Throughput/cost means output tokens/s, requests/s, and cost per 1M output tokens; report energy only if measured.

**Traffic and matrix.** Use open-loop arrivals independent of server latency. Poisson is a weak default; include bursty/Markov-modulated or real traces and multi-turn/agentic interleaved prefills. Cover ISL/OSL distributions, prefix-reuse rate, long context, dense versus MoE, reasoning/long output, BF16/FP8/FP4, TP/PP/EP/DP, block size, chunk size, `max_num_tokens`, KV budget, cache on/off, and SLO targets. Compare colocated, chunked-prefill, and disaggregated configurations at the same GPU count and attainment.

**Reproducibility and ablations.** Pin engine version/commit, CUDA/driver, GPU SKU and clocks/power, model/tokenizer revision, quantization, parallelism, block size, batch-token cap, chunk size, scheduling policy, cache state, seed, trace hash, warmup, run count, and percentile method; publish raw latency distributions. Keep kernel microbenchmarks (TFLOP/s, bandwidth, occupancy) separate from end-to-end serving. Ablate colocated versus disaggregated, chunked prefill versus disagg, prefix caching on/off, TP versus PP, EP size and redundant experts, attention backend, and scheduler policy. Inject KV-transfer timeout/corruption, prefill or decode crash, L3 outage, overload/429, cancellation storms, and duplicate IDs; measure recovery time and SLO attainment during failure. Note that greedy outputs can change across batch compositions because kernels reassociate floating point, and that prompt-cache reuse can change logits [22][23].

## 9. Recommendations

1. **Start colocated** with chunked prefill and prefix caching; measure SLO attainment before paying for disaggregation.
2. **Disaggregate only on evidence:** conflicting TTFT/TPOT SLOs, long prompts or high reuse, and NVLink or ≥100 Gbps RDMA. Treat it as tail-latency isolation, not a throughput feature.
3. **Pick kernels by phase and hardware:** FA3/FA4 for prefill/long context on Hopper/Blackwell; FlashInfer/FA2-class paged/varlen kernels or XQA for decode. Pin versions and shapes.
4. **Invest in the cache before the split:** local radix/prefix cache, KV-aware routing, then a measured L3. Enforce tenant isolation and block-identity correctness.
5. **Map parallelism to topology:** TP inside NVLink, PP across nodes, EP with load balancing for MoE, CP only for long prefill, DP plus KV-aware routing for replicas.
6. **Report SLO-goodput at p95/p99 under open-loop traffic with pinned configurations.** A throughput number without SLOs, hardware, workload, and comparator is not comparable.
